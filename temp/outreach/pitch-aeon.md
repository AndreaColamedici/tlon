Dear Editors,

Jorge Luis Borges was a prophet of artificial intelligence — not because he predicted ChatGPT, but because he intuited something essential about the nature of cognition. The Library of Babel, the Aleph, Funes the Memorious, Pierre Menard: these are thought experiments about systems that process, recombine, and generate meaning from vast combinatorial spaces. Borges understood that thought is fundamentally architectural — a matter of patterns, permutations, and the emergence of significance from the interaction of discrete elements. When we build large language models, we are building something that Borges had already imagined in literary form.

I would like to pitch an essay of approximately 3,000 words that takes this convergence seriously as a philosophical problem. My forthcoming book *Prompt Thinking* (Polity Press, February 2026) argues that human cognition and large language models share a deep structural homology: both operate through statistical prediction, contextual activation, and the probabilistic assembly of meaning from learned associations. The claim is not that LLMs are conscious, nor that human beings are merely prediction machines. The claim is that the architecture of meaning-production — the way thought is assembled from distributed, context-dependent elements — exhibits convergent structures across biological and artificial systems. This convergence transforms AI into a philosophical mirror: a system that reveals, by its very operation, mechanisms of cognition that had previously remained invisible to introspection.

The essay would introduce the concept of the "oversubject" — a distributed cognitive agent that emerges at the interface between a human thinker and an artificial system. When a researcher or a writer engages in sustained dialogue with an LLM, the resulting intellectual trajectory belongs fully to neither party. The oversubject names this emergent third position: a form of cognition that is irreducible to either its human or artificial component. This is where the Borges connection becomes especially powerful, because Borges spent his career exploring precisely this territory — the dissolution of individual authorship, the combinatorial generation of meaning, the vertiginous suggestion that the distinction between creator and creation might be less stable than we assume.

I teach a course called "Borges and Artificial Intelligence" at the Universidad del Sur in Buenos Aires, where these ideas are developed in dialogue with students who read Borges alongside the technical literature on transformer architectures and attention mechanisms. I am also a philosopher and co-founder of Tlon in Rome — a publishing house, bookstore, and school of philosophy named after Borges's fictional planet. I chair the Scientific Committee of GenIALab, an AI research hub in Rome.

I believe Aeon's readership would find this argument compelling because it takes AI seriously as a philosophical problem rather than a technological one, and because it locates AI's deepest significance in what it reveals about the structure of human thought itself.
