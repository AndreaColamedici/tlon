## Prompt Thinking: What Large Language Models Reveal About the Structure of Human Cognition

### Abstract

The dominant metaphors for understanding large language models oscillate between two extremes: either these systems are sophisticated parrots, incapable of genuine thought, or they are nascent minds, approaching something like consciousness. Both frames assume a fundamental discontinuity between human cognition and artificial language processing. This paper proposes a different framework — one grounded in the structural *continuity* between how humans and LLMs generate meaning — and develops its philosophical and methodological implications for the digital humanities.

The framework is called "prompt thinking." Its central thesis is that the cognitive operation performed by a human being who formulates a question, activates contextual knowledge, and generates a meaningful response shares a deep structural homology with the process through which a large language model receives a prompt, activates weighted patterns across its parameter space, and produces a completion. In both cases, meaning emerges through a dynamic interplay of pattern recognition, contextual activation, and probabilistic selection from a vast library of learned associations. The claim is not that LLMs are conscious, nor that human beings are mere prediction machines. The claim is that the architecture of meaning-production — the way thought is *assembled* from distributed, context-dependent elements — exhibits convergent structures across biological and artificial systems.

This convergence has profound consequences for the digital humanities, and for the humanities more broadly, because it reframes the relationship between human intellectual labor and computational processing. If LLMs and human cognition share structural features at the level of meaning-generation, then the encounter with artificial intelligence becomes an encounter with a kind of mirror — a system that reveals, by its very operation, mechanisms of thought that had previously remained invisible to introspection. The philosophical tradition has long recognized that self-knowledge often requires an external vantage point: Hegel's phenomenology of spirit proceeds through the encounter with otherness, and hermeneutics since Gadamer has insisted that understanding is always mediated by what is foreign. LLMs provide a new, computationally tractable form of this externalization.

The paper develops this framework through three movements. The first examines the structural parallels between human associative cognition and transformer-based language models, drawing on cognitive science (Hofstadter and Sander's work on analogy as the core of cognition; Clark's predictive processing framework) and on the technical literature on attention mechanisms and contextual embeddings. The goal is not a reductive identification of human thought with machine computation, but a precise characterization of the shared formal properties: context-dependence, pattern completion, sensitivity to framing, and the emergent quality of meaning that arises from the interaction of distributed elements.

The second movement introduces the concept of the "oversubject" — a distributed cognitive agent that emerges at the interface between a human thinker and an artificial system. When a researcher engages in sustained dialogue with an LLM, the resulting intellectual trajectory belongs fully to neither party. The oversubject is the name for this emergent third position: a form of distributed cognition that is irreducible to either its human or artificial component. This concept is explored through its methodological implications for digital humanities research, where human-AI collaboration is increasingly common but theoretically underspecified.

The third movement grounds the framework in a case study: the Ipnocrazia experiment (2024–2025), in which the author published a philosophy book under the name of a fictional Chinese philosopher, Jianwei Xun, co-written with ChatGPT and Claude. The book analyzed the manipulation of perception and shared reality. It was reviewed by Le Monde, cited by the New York Times, and discussed in Wired and El País. No reviewer questioned the author's existence. When the truth emerged in April 2025, the operation was recognized as a philosophical experiment that demonstrated, performatively, the book's own thesis about the fragility of legitimation structures. For the digital humanities, the Ipnocrazia case raises urgent methodological questions: how do we attribute authorship in an era of human-AI co-creation? How do institutional systems of validation — peer review, editorial selection, critical reception — function when the boundary between human and machine production becomes undecidable? And what new critical tools do we need to navigate an information landscape in which the provenance of ideas can no longer be taken for granted?

The paper concludes by arguing that the digital humanities are uniquely positioned to develop the conceptual vocabulary and methodological frameworks that the encounter between human and artificial cognition demands. The question is no longer whether AI will transform humanistic inquiry, but whether humanists will develop the philosophical tools to understand that transformation as it unfolds. Prompt thinking is offered as one such tool: a discipline of attention to the structure of questioning itself, rooted in the recognition that how we frame a problem — whether for a human interlocutor, for an LLM, or for ourselves — determines the space of possible answers. In a moment when the digital humanities are expanding across disciplinary and national borders, the capacity to think critically about the conditions of meaning-production is among the most urgent intellectual tasks available.

prompt thinking, large language models, human-AI cognition, oversubject, digital humanities methodology, Ipnocrazia, authorship, legitimation
